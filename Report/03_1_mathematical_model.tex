\subsection{Mathematical Model}
\label{sec:model}

We compare an initial seven-feature representation with an expanded representation containing 82 acoustic measurements. Ordinary least squares and ridge regression provide interpretable starting points. Nonlinear models and an LSTM examine additional relationships within the same grouped evaluation framework.

\subsubsection{Predictors and responses}

For recording $i$, let $v_i$ and $a_i$ denote its supplied valence and arousal scores. We fit separate static regression models for these responses. In the equations below, $y_i$ represents either response.

Let $x_{ij}$ denote acoustic feature $j$ for recording $i$, where $j=1,\ldots,p$. The initial seven features are mean and standard deviation of RMS amplitude, mean spectral centroid, bass-band power fraction, positive spectral flux, onsets per second, and estimated tempo.

The expanded representation adds 75 measurements, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:features}. Chroma describes the distribution of spectral energy among pitch classes \cite{librosachroma}. Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and spectral contrast provide additional summaries of spectral shape and timbre.

These features describe acoustic properties. RMS amplitude is an approximate loudness-related quantity, and estimated tempo can contain half-time or double-time errors. Several measurements also describe overlapping information, which motivates the use of regularization.

One descriptive pitch feature compares the strongest major and minor triad-template matches. Let $C_0,\ldots,C_{11}$ be average chroma values. We define
\begin{equation}
H=
\frac{
\max_r(C_r+C_{r+4}+C_{r+7})
-
\max_r(C_r+C_{r+3}+C_{r+7})
}{
\sum_{k=0}^{11}C_k+10^{-12}
},
\label{eq:triad}
\end{equation}
where indices are taken modulo 12. This summarizes the overall pitch-class distribution. It should be interpreted as a descriptive template contrast rather than a verified key estimate or chord transcription.

Because the predictors have different units, each feature is standardized:
\begin{equation}
z_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-\mu_j}{s_j},
\label{eq:standardization}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_j$ and $s_j$ are calculated from the training partition. Validation and test recordings use these same training values. Features with no training variation are removed.

A one-unit increase in $z_{ij}$ corresponds to one training standard deviation in the original measurement. Standardization also prevents the original units from determining the strength of the ridge penalty.

\subsubsection{Ordinary least squares}

Our linear model is
\begin{equation}
y_i=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jz_{ij}+\varepsilon_i.
\label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
The intercept is the prediction at the training-average feature values. Each coefficient describes the change in prediction associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in that feature, holding the other included features fixed. The residual represents variation not captured by the model.

For $m$ training recordings, ordinary least squares minimizes
\begin{equation}
\min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jz_{ij}\right)^2.
\label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
Squared errors prevent positive and negative residuals from canceling and give larger errors more weight.

Let $Z$ be the standardized feature matrix and let
\[
\mathbf y_c=(y_1-\bar y,\ldots,y_m-\bar y)^\top
\]
be the centered response vector. Centering the predictors gives the fitted intercept $\widehat\beta_0=\bar y$. Differentiating the objective gives the normal equations
\begin{equation}
Z^\top Z\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}=Z^\top\mathbf y_c.
\end{equation}
If the columns of $Z$ are linearly independent, then
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}}
=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top\mathbf y_c.
\end{equation}
The implementation uses a numerical least-squares solver rather than explicitly calculating this inverse.

\subsubsection{Ridge regression}

Correlated predictors can make ordinary least-squares coefficients sensitive to the training sample. Ridge regression addresses this by adding a squared-coefficient penalty \cite{vanwieringen2026ridge}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}
\left\{
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_jz_{ij}\right)^2
+
\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2
\right\}.
\label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
The intercept is unpenalized. When $\lambda=0$, the objective reduces to ordinary least squares. Increasing $\lambda$ shrinks the feature coefficients toward zero, which can reduce sensitivity to the training sample.

For centered responses and standardized predictors,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\mathrm{ridge}}
=
(Z^\top Z+m\lambda I)^{-1}Z^\top\mathbf y_c.
\label{eq:ridge_solution}
\end{equation}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:ridge_unique}
For $\lambda>0$, ridge regression has a unique solution even when the predictors are linearly dependent.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For any nonzero vector $\mathbf u$,
\[
\mathbf u^\top(Z^\top Z+m\lambda I)\mathbf u
=
\|Z\mathbf u\|_2^2+m\lambda\|\mathbf u\|_2^2>0.
\]
Thus $Z^\top Z+m\lambda I$ is positive definite and invertible. Equation~\eqref{eq:ridge_solution} gives a unique coefficient vector. Since the training predictors are centered, the unpenalized intercept equals the training-response mean.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:ridge_unique} explains why ridge remains well defined when measurements contain redundant information. The penalty still needs to be selected through validation because excessive shrinkage can remove useful predictive information.

The seven-feature ridge model uses penalties from $10^{-4}$ through $10^4$. The expanded model uses penalties from $10^{-4}$ through $10^2$. Both grids are logarithmically spaced. For software using a sum of squared errors instead of the average-error objective, the equivalent penalty is $\alpha=m\lambda$.

\subsubsection{Nonlinear comparisons}

A random forest averages predictions from multiple regression trees \cite{sklearnforest}. This permits nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors. We use 100 trees and select the minimum leaf size and feature fraction through inner validation.

We also evaluate an approximation to radial basis function (RBF) kernel ridge regression. This maps standardized measurements into a nonlinear representation and fits ridge regression to that representation. A 64-landmark Nystr\"om approximation limits the computational cost \cite{sklearnnystroem}. The mapping is fitted within each training partition, and its kernel width and penalty are selected through inner validation.

These models test whether more flexible relationships improve prediction. Their outputs require a different interpretation from the exact coefficient decomposition available for a linear model.

\subsubsection{Long short-term memory model}

The LSTM processes ten consecutive windows from each recording. Each window contains RMS amplitude, spectral centroid, bass-band content, and onset rate.

Its internal states update according to
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf h_{it},\mathbf c_{it})
=
\operatorname{LSTM}_{\theta}
(\mathbf z_{it},\mathbf h_{i,t-1},\mathbf c_{i,t-1}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf h_{it}$ is a hidden representation and $\mathbf c_{it}$ is a memory state \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}. These states allow measurements from earlier windows to influence the final representation.

Two output layers predict the overall responses:
\begin{align}
\widehat v_i &= b_v+\mathbf w_v^\top\mathbf h_{iT},\\
\widehat a_i &= b_a+\mathbf w_a^\top\mathbf h_{iT}.
\end{align}
Training minimizes average squared error across both responses:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=
\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\left[
(v_i-\widehat v_i)^2+
(a_i-\widehat a_i)^2
\right].
\end{equation}

The evaluated network has eight hidden units and trains for 120 full-batch Adam epochs, with gradient clipping and weight regularization. These settings are fixed rather than selected from outer test scores.

The LSTM uses four window-level measurements, while the expanded static models use 82 excerpt-level summaries. Their comparison therefore changes both the model and its representation. The available targets are static labels, so evaluation concerns overall emotion prediction.

\subsubsection{Evaluation and feature interpretation}

We use five outer folds for evaluation and three inner folds for parameter selection. Recordings are grouped by normalized complete artist-credit strings. All measurements and windows from a recording remain in the same group.

The baseline predicts the outer training mean. We report pooled out-of-fold prediction errors:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{RMSE}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
(y_i-\widehat y_i^{\mathrm{OOF}})^2
},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
R^2=
1-
\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i^{\mathrm{OOF}})^2}
{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
Each out-of-fold prediction comes from a model that excluded the corresponding artist-credit group. The mean $\bar y$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:r2} is calculated over the evaluated responses.

For a linear model, the prediction can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
\widehat y_i-\widehat\beta_0
=
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\widehat\beta_jz_{ij}.
\label{eq:contributions}
\end{equation}
Each term describes a feature's contribution relative to the intercept.

We also remove feature families, refit and retune ridge, and repeat evaluation:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\operatorname{RMSE}
=
\operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{reduced}}
-
\operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{full}}.
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate worse prediction after removal. These comparisons describe predictive usefulness conditional on the retained measurements.
