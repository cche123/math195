\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A recording can have a strong rhythmic pulse and still sound sad. This combination suggests that musical intensity and emotional positivity are distinct aspects of its emotional character. Examining them separately provides a way to study how measurable properties of sound relate to perceived emotion.

Our research question is:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{How do acoustic features relate to the emotions we hear in music?}
\end{quote}

We represent emotional character through valence and arousal. Valence describes emotional positivity, while arousal describes activation. These dimensions allow a recording to have low valence and high arousal, giving us a framework for studying negative emotional character in music with an active beat.

The project initially focused on electronic dance music (EDM). Reviewing the candidate recordings showed that broad electronic and dance-related metadata categories did not consistently match our intended genre definition. The limited sample meeting that definition motivated a broader study across genres. MERGE remains our primary dataset, and the revised analysis retains the same interest in interpretable acoustic measurements.

The initial pilot used seven features describing amplitude, rhythm, and spectral content. Those models improved arousal prediction modestly but did not improve valence prediction over a training-mean baseline. We therefore examined a broader sample and added measurements of timbre and pitch-class content. The follow-up compares the original and expanded representations on the same eligible recordings.

The results support prediction of both responses, although substantial variation remains unexplained. Expanded ridge regression connects individual measurements to predictions, while the random forest tests whether nonlinear relationships improve performance. A small long short-term memory network provides an additional comparison using ordered acoustic measurements.

Two case studies will connect these findings to recordings selected for contrasting perceived sadness and happiness. We will examine predicted valence and arousal, the features contributing to the ridge predictions, and differences between manually identified sections. These examples will help us assess how the statistical relationships correspond to observations about the music.

Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the relevant literature. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} develops the mathematical models and describes the implementation. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the completed analysis, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses its implications and the remaining work.
