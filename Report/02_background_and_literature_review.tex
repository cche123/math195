\section{Background and Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

Music emotion recognition examines relationships between information in a recording and its emotional character. Previous work informs our definition of emotion, choice of data, and interpretation of predictions. Our study emphasizes acoustic measurements with recognizable meanings while testing whether their relationships with emotion extend to held-out recordings.

\subsection{Defining the emotional response}

Kang and Herremans distinguish perceived emotion from induced emotion in their survey of music emotion prediction \cite[Secs.~II and V]{kang2025survey}. Perceived emotion describes the emotional character heard in a recording. Induced emotion describes the listener's own response. These targets can differ: a listener may enjoy music perceived as sad.

The survey also distinguishes static annotations, which summarize a recording or excerpt, from dynamic annotations that follow changes over time. This distinction determines the claims our analysis can support. A model trained on an overall label can produce predictions for shorter sections, but interpreting those predictions as measurements of emotion within each section requires additional validation.

Valence and arousal provide numerical responses suited to regression. Tempo and amplitude are acoustic predictors, while arousal is a supplied emotion label. Similarly, low valence can correspond to sadness, anger, or other negative emotions. We therefore interpret the numerical predictions as valence and arousal, using written listening descriptions to discuss sadness more specifically in the case studies.

\subsection{MERGE and its annotation framework}

Louro et al.\ introduce MERGE for static emotion prediction using audio, lyrics, and both modalities together \cite[Secs.~III--V]{louro2025merge}. The complete audio release contains 3,554 excerpts, while the bimodal dataset contains 2,216 examples. The paper evaluates four-quadrant classification and numerical valence--arousal regression.

MERGE's construction process maps AllMusic emotion tags into the valence--arousal plane using affective word ratings and includes manual validation. Examples close to the center are excluded to reduce ambiguity. The numerical responses therefore reflect the dataset's annotation procedure and selection criteria.

These details matter for our interpretation. Our models predict the supplied labels, and their performance must be understood in relation to how those labels were constructed. Excluding central examples may also affect how well the fitted relationships describe recordings with less clearly defined emotional character.

MERGE provides an established framework for comparing audio-based prediction methods. Our project uses that framework to examine interpretable measurements and their application to selected recordings. Mood tags and other information derived from the responses are excluded from the predictors.

\subsection{Emotion prediction from separate musical sources}

In EmoMucs, de Berardinis, Cangelosi, and Coutinho compare emotion prediction from full recordings with prediction from estimated vocals, bass, drums, and other accompaniment \cite[Secs.~3--4]{deberardinis2020emomucs}. They fit source-specific models and investigate ways to combine their predictions.

Using PMEmo's static induced-emotion annotations, one configuration improves valence $R^2$ from approximately $0.414$ for the full-mix baseline to $0.481$ after combining source predictions. Arousal performance is comparable to the baseline. Individual sources generally perform less well than the complete recording, and the most informative source varies across tracks.

These findings suggest that emotion-related information can be distributed across several components. They also support retaining a full-mix baseline, since isolating a source can remove relationships audible when the components are heard together.

The annotation target differs from ours, so the reported scores provide methodological context rather than a directly comparable benchmark. Our measurements describe the complete mix. For example, low-frequency power can contain bass instruments, kick drums, and other sounds. We therefore describe this measurement as bass-band content rather than attributing it to a single instrument.

\subsection{Source separation and learned representations}

Hybrid Transformer Demucs estimates musical sources using waveform and spectrogram information \cite{rouard2023demucs}. It could support a later comparison of features extracted from estimated vocals, bass, drums, and accompaniment. Its reconstruction task is distinct from predicting emotion.

Estimated sources may contain leakage or distortion, particularly when sounds occupy overlapping frequency ranges. Representative outputs would need to be inspected before interpreting their features as instrument-specific evidence. Demucs has not been used in the completed analysis.

MERT offers an alternative through numerical representations learned from music using self-supervised training \cite{li2024mert}. These representations can support downstream prediction tasks and may capture information beyond a manually selected feature set.

The tradeoff for our study is interpretation. Measurements such as spectral centroid and onset rate have direct acoustic meanings, while individual representation coordinates generally do not. We therefore use explicit features extracted with librosa. MERT and Essentia remain possible options for subsequent comparisons.

\subsection{Musical structure and acoustic change}

Solberg examines production techniques in EDM buildups and drops, relating changes in rhythm, frequency content, and bass to expectation and emotional intensity \cite{solberg2014bass}. This work motivated our original interest in breakdowns, buildups, and drops.

Its relevance to the revised project is the possibility that acoustic change carries information beyond average feature values. Two excerpts can have similar mean amplitude while developing differently over time. An excerpt-level average does not preserve that order.

Section descriptions will depend on the recording. The case studies may involve verses, choruses, bridges, breakdowns, or drops where appropriate. Solberg's analysis motivates these comparisons without establishing a common structure across genres.

Long short-term memory networks provide a way to process ordered measurements \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}. We evaluate a small LSTM using consecutive windows of acoustic features. Its target remains the overall excerpt label, so evaluation concerns prediction of the recording-level response.

\subsection{DEAM and implications for evaluation}

DEAM contains 1,802 excerpts and full songs with recording-level and time-varying valence and arousal annotations \cite{deamdataset}. Its temporal labels make it relevant to questions about emotional changes within a recording.

We have not combined DEAM with MERGE. Pooling them would require checking repeated recordings, audio availability, annotation targets, and label scales. Matching numerical ranges would not by itself establish that the labels measure the same response.

Together, these studies support an analysis that distinguishes predictive performance from musical interpretation. Held-out evaluation tests whether the acoustic relationships extend beyond the training recordings. The case studies will then examine how those relationships correspond to observations about individual songs.
